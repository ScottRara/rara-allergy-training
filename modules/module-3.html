Identifying Allergens at RaRa Ramen

Learning Objective: Know Australia's mandatory declarable allergens in detail, understand where they hide in common ramen ingredients, and learn to use the RaRa Ramen allergen menu matrix.

Australia's Mandatory Declarable Allergens

ANY food can cause an allergic reaction, including anaphylaxis. However, the following foods cause 90% of allergic reactions in Australia. These must be declared on food labels or communicated to customers.

🍜
Wheat / Gluten
Noodles, soy sauce, breadcrumbs, gyoza wrappers
🥜
Eggs
Ramen noodles, ajitama eggs, tempura batter
🥛
Milk (Dairy)
Butter, cream, cheese, some sauces
🥜
Soy
Soy sauce, tofu, miso, edamame, soybean oil
🍄
Sesame
Sesame oil, sesame seeds, tahini
🦐
Peanuts
Peanut oil, satay, some Asian sauces
🌳
Tree Nuts
Cashew, almond, walnut — listed individually under PEAL
🐟
Fish
Dashi, fish sauce, bonito flakes, fish stock
🦐
Crustaceans
Prawns, crab, lobster, shrimp paste
🦐
Molluscs
Squid, octopus, oyster sauce, mussels

Additional mandatory allergens: Lupin (a legume found in some breads and baked goods) and Sulphites (in some dried foods and wines, when added at 10mg/kg or more).

Common Allergens in Ramen Dishes

Ramen is a high-risk food for allergens because it typically contains multiple mandatory allergens in a single bowl. Here's where they hide at RaRa (but familiarise yourself with the allergen matrix for the correct allergens):

Hidden Allergens Some allergens are not obvious. Fish sauce, oyster sauce, Worcestershire sauce, and Caesar dressing all contain fish. Dark chocolate usually contains milk. Soy is in many processed foods. Always check — never assume.

Allergen Deep Dives — What You Need to Know

Peanuts & Tree Nuts

Peanut is a legume (not a tree nut). Under PEAL, tree nuts must be listed by their individual names (almond, cashew, hazelnut, etc.) — the generic term "tree nut" is no longer permitted on labels. Some people with peanut allergy are also allergic to lupin. At RaRa, always check if peanut oil or nut garnishes are used in any dish.

Sesame

Sesame is extremely common in ramen cuisine — as sesame oil, sesame seeds, and tahini. Both black and white sesame seeds are allergens. Sesame oil is used in many dressings, marinades, and finishing oils at RaRa.

Soy

Soy is in nearly everything at a ramen restaurant: soy sauce, miso, tofu, edamame, soybean oil, and many processed ingredients. It can be an unexpected ingredient in bread, processed meats, and sauces.

Fish & Seafood

Most people with fish allergy are allergic to all types of fish and may also react to crustaceans and molluscs. At RaRa, dashi (fish stock) is a base for many broths. Fish sauce and bonito flakes are common ingredients. Always ask if a customer is allergic to all seafood or specific types.

Crustaceans & Molluscs

Crustaceans (prawns, crab, lobster) and molluscs (squid, octopus, oysters, mussels) are common in Asian cuisine. Oyster sauce is a hidden source of mollusc allergen. Some people allergic to crustaceans are also allergic to molluscs and fish — always ask.

Using the RaRa Ramen Allergen Menu Matrix

The allergen menu matrix is your most important tool for answering customer allergen questions. It lists every menu item and shows which mandatory allergens are present.

How to Use the Matrix

  1. Find the menu item the customer is asking about
  2. Look across the row to see which allergens are marked
  3. Communicate this clearly to the customer
  4. If the customer's allergen is marked, the dish is NOT safe for them
  5. Help the customer find an alternative or discuss modifications
Keeping the Matrix Current The allergen matrix must be updated every time a recipe changes, a new ingredient is used, or a supplier changes their product. This is a Food Safety Supervisor responsibility, but all staff should report any ingredient changes they notice.

Tricky Situations at RaRa

Scenario: Deep Fryer Cross-Contamination

A customer with a wheat and egg allergy orders karaage chicken. The chicken has been coated in flour and egg wash, so the dish itself is unsafe. But even if you had a wheat-free, egg-free version, the deep fryer oil may be contaminated with wheat and egg from previous batches. You would need to use fresh oil or a separate fryer.

Scenario: Soy in Everything

A customer declares a soy allergy. At a ramen restaurant, this is extremely challenging because soy sauce is in most broths, marinades, and tare. You must check the allergen matrix carefully and be honest with the customer about which dishes can be safely modified and which cannot.

Golden Rule You cannot simply "pick off" or "remove" an allergen from a completed dish. If an egg has been placed on a ramen bowl, you cannot remove the egg and serve the rest — cross-contamination has already occurred. Start the meal again.

Knowledge Check

1. How many mandatory food allergens must be declared in Australia?

Correct! Australia has an extensive list of mandatory declarable allergens covering the major allergen groups.
Not quite. Australia requires declaration of peanuts, tree nuts (individually named), milk, eggs, wheat/gluten, soy, fish, crustaceans, molluscs, sesame, lupin, and sulphites.

2. A customer with a fish allergy orders tonkotsu ramen. What must you check?

Correct! Even though tonkotsu is pork-based, the broth may contain dashi, fish sauce, or bonito as flavour enhancers. Always check the recipe and allergen matrix.
Incorrect. Many ramen broths, even pork-based ones, contain hidden fish ingredients like dashi, fish sauce, or bonito flakes. You must always check the full recipe.

3. An egg was accidentally placed on a bowl for a customer with an egg allergy. You remove it. Is the ramen safe to serve?

Correct! Once an allergen has touched the food, cross-contamination has occurred. You must start the meal again from scratch.
Incorrect. Removing the allergen is NOT enough. Even a tiny residue can cause a reaction. The meal must be started again with clean equipment.

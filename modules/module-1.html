Module 1

Introduction to Allergen Awareness

Learning Objective: Understand food allergies as a critical safety issue, learn why allergen management is a shared responsibility, and recognise the foundations of best practice at RaRa Ramen.

What is a Food Allergy?

A food allergy is an immune system reaction to a protein in food. When someone with a food allergy eats even a tiny amount of their allergen, their body treats it as a threat and mounts a defensive response that can range from mild symptoms to life-threatening anaphylaxis.

Critical Fact There is no cure for food allergy. The only way to manage it is to completely avoid the allergen. Even a drop or crumb can cause a life-threatening reaction. Heating, cooking, or freezing does NOT destroy allergens.

Key Statistics

  • 1 in 10 babies have a food allergy
  • 1 in 20 children have a food allergy
  • 1 in 50 adults have a food allergy
  • Most deaths from food allergies happen outside the home — in businesses like RaRa Ramen

Food Allergy vs Food Intolerance

It is essential that kitchen staff understand the difference between a food allergy and a food intolerance, as they require different levels of response — but both must be taken seriously.

Food Allergy Food Intolerance
Cause Immune system reaction to a protein Difficulty digesting a food component
Severity Can be life-threatening (anaphylaxis) Uncomfortable but not life-threatening
Amount needed Even traces can trigger a reaction Usually depends on quantity consumed
Examples Peanut allergy, milk allergy, egg allergy Lactose intolerance, coeliac disease
Response Complete avoidance & separate preparation Avoid the ingredient; cross-contact less critical (but still important)
RaRa Ramen Policy At RaRa, we treat ALL allergy and intolerance requests with the highest level of care. Never assume a request is "just an intolerance" — always follow full allergen-safe preparation procedures.

Spotlight: Wheat Allergy vs Coeliac Disease

Wheat allergy is an immune reaction to any protein in wheat (not just gluten). People with wheat allergy must avoid wheat but may eat other gluten-containing grains like rye or barley if not allergic to them.

Coeliac disease is an autoimmune condition where consuming gluten (found in wheat, rye, oats, and barley) causes damage to the small intestine. People with coeliac disease must avoid ALL gluten, and even trace amounts from cross-contamination can cause harm.

Spotlight: Milk Allergy vs Lactose Intolerance

Milk allergy is an immune reaction to milk protein and can cause anaphylaxis. People with milk allergy must avoid ALL dairy products, including lactose-free dairy (which still contains milk proteins).

Lactose intolerance is the inability to digest lactose (milk sugar), causing digestive discomfort but NOT life-threatening reactions. Lactose-free products are safe for people with lactose intolerance.

Common Mistake Giving someone with a milk allergy a lactose-free product is DANGEROUS. Lactose-free dairy still contains milk proteins that can trigger anaphylaxis.

Allergen Management is a Shared Responsibility

Everyone in the food chain has a role to play in preventing allergic reactions. At RaRa Ramen, this means every single team member — from the kitchen hand to the store manager.

Who is Responsible?

WhoTheir Responsibility
Customers Must tell staff about their food allergy when ordering
Front-of-House Staff Listen carefully, confirm allergies, communicate accurately to kitchen
Kitchen Staff Know the allergen matrix, prevent cross-contamination, follow standardised recipes
Food Safety Supervisor Ensure the business manages allergen risks, train staff, keep records updated
Legislators & Regulators Set and enforce food allergen laws and regulations
Legal Warning Under state and territory Food Acts, it is an offence to fail to supply food in the manner demanded by the purchaser (if the business accepts the request). Not knowing about food allergens is not an excuse.

Best Practice at RaRa Ramen

Best practice allergen management at RaRa Ramen rests on three pillars:

1
Communication Always treat allergy requests seriously. Communication between front-of-house and kitchen about food allergies is crucial. Promote a strong food safety culture.
2
Know What's in Your Food Understand how to identify allergens in packaged and unpackaged ingredients. Check ingredients EVERY time. Never substitute ingredients without checking allergen content. Keep the allergen menu matrix up to date.
3
Prevent Cross-Contamination Have procedures for storing, preparing, and serving food that prevent cross-contact. Ensure equipment and surfaces are cleaned (not just sanitised). Wash hands before, after, and between each food preparation task.

Knowledge Check

Test your understanding before moving to Module 2.

1. A customer says they have a milk allergy. Which of the following is safe to serve them?

Correct! Soy milk (if they're not also allergic to soy) does not contain milk proteins. Lactose-free, low-fat dairy, and goat's milk all contain milk proteins.
Not quite. Lactose-free dairy, low-fat dairy, and goat's milk all still contain milk proteins that can trigger anaphylaxis. Soy milk (if they're not allergic to soy) would be the safe option.

2. Does cooking or heating food destroy allergens?

Correct! Heating, cooking, or freezing does NOT destroy allergens. The food remains unsafe for a person with that allergy.
Incorrect. Allergens are proteins that are NOT destroyed by heat, cooking, or freezing. The food remains unsafe regardless of how it's prepared.

3. Whose responsibility is it to manage food allergen risks?

Correct! Allergen management is a shared responsibility across the entire food chain.
Not quite. Allergen management is a SHARED responsibility — customers, front-of-house, kitchen, supervisors, and regulators all play a role.
